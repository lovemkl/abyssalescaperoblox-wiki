Abyssal Escape's survivor side is a coordination game wearing a horror skin, and this page is the coordination playbook: mist priority and dispel discipline, the team-role distribution that launch-week squads are converging on, killer-specific survival adjustments and the mistake catalog that ends runs before the endgame. It assumes you've read the how-to-play guide's basics; this page is the layer above.

## Mist Priority: The Discipline That Wins Rounds

The survivor side's core skill: treating the mist's advance as the round's true clock and disciplining the team's dispel effort accordingly. The principles, in priority order. **Dispel proactively, not reactively**: the teams that maintain steady dispel pressure through the mid-game hold the map's playable space open for the endgame; the teams that let the mist run and try to claw space back late discover the mist's snowball — reclaimed ground is expensive, and the endgame's space constraints make the reclamation nearly impossible. **Dispel where you'll play**: dispel effort aimed at space the team will actually use (objective routes, rotation lanes, the escape approach) beats dispel effort aimed at space that merely looks threatened — the mist punishes wasted labor as surely as no labor. **Dispel under cover of role distribution**: the mistake that loses mid-games is four survivors dispelling together (safe but slow — the objectives starve) or none dispelling while all four run objectives (fast but doomed — the mist wins the map race). The balance is roles: someone owns the mist front so the others can fight theirs.

## Team Roles: The Launch-Week Meta

The four-role distribution that launch-week squads are converging on, worth naming because it converts the game's three-front attention economy into assignable jobs. **The Anchor**: the mist owner — the survivor whose primary job is dispel discipline, holding the map's playable space and calling the mist's advance like a shot-caller. **The Runner**: the objective specialist — fastest on the team, comfortable with exposure, whose job is progress while others hold space. **The Support**: the mid-role — flexible between dispel and objectives, plugging whichever front sags, and the default rescue-initiator when the Killer lands a down. **The Bait**: the chase-capable survivor — comfortable being seen, whose job when the Killer pressures is to waste the Killer's time productively (the mist keeps advancing while the chase burns). The roles are porous — good teams flex constantly — but the launch-week evidence is clear: teams that distribute the three fronts across named roles outperform teams of four interchangeable solo-operators, and the margin is not small.

## Killer-Specific Adjustments

The matchup layer, condensed from the killer roster page. **Against The Maniac**: the fundamentals matchup — mist discipline wins his rounds, so anchor harder, dispel steadier, and treat his chases as the team's free time (every second he chases is a second the other three progress). His phase escalation punishes endgame greed — the escape choke is his kill-window, so stagger the approach rather than funneling. **Against The Furnace**: the positioning matchup — his zone control punishes predictable routing, so route discipline wins his rounds: rotate through the space he isn't controlling, respect his phases' area denial (the objective zones he matures into kill-boxes deserve wide berths in his late phases), and let his area pressure work on empty rooms while the team plays the map he isn't in. The matchup summaries simplify — the roster page carries the full profiles — but the pattern holds across the roster: each killer teaches a different survival discipline, and the team that adjusts per-killer rather than playing one static style banks the difference.

## The Mistake Catalog

The survivor mistakes that end runs, in rough order of how often launch-week rounds die to them. **Passive mist play** (the number one killer of launch-week rounds — teams that hide well and dispel nothing lose to the clock they ignored). **The four-man ball** (safe, slow and doomed — balling up clears zero objectives and eventually feeds the killer a multi-down; spread with roles, not with fear). **Objective-greed at the choke** (the endgame's escape approach is the round's kill-window — treating it as a victory lap is how 3-escape rounds become 0-escape rounds; stagger, cover and commit). **The unproductive chase** (being chased is fine — being chased away from where your team needs space is the loss; bait on your terms, toward the mist you're holding, not away from it). **The solo rescue** (the rescue is a team action timed against the Killer's patrol, not a heroic sprint; solo rescue attempts feed double-downs). Five mistakes, five habits to unlearn — and the launch-week meta's genuine skill floor is mostly this catalog avoided.

## The Closing Triage

The summary skill that contains all the others: triage. At every moment of every round, the survivor side is choosing between the mist front, the objective front and the threat front — and the game's depth is that the right choice changes every thirty seconds. Roles give the team a default distribution; communication updates it; the strategy pages' principles guide it. The teams that escape consistently in the launch window are not the teams with the best chasers or the fastest runners — they are the teams whose triage is always correct, whose mist never runs untended, whose objectives never starve and whose threat responses never cost more than they save. Coordinate, dispel, escape. The mist is patient. Be more patient.
